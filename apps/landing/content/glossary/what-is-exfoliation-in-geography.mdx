## What is Exfoliation in Geography?

Exfoliation in geography is a geological process where thin layers or sheets of rock separate from the main rock body, often due to pressure release. This natural phenomenon is most commonly observed in regions with moderate rainfall and is prevalent in upland areas with exposures of uniform coarsely crystalline igneous rocks, such as granite or basalt.

## How It Works

Exfoliation occurs primarily due to the release of pressure and chemical weathering, rather than thermal expansion and contraction as previously believed. When the overlying rock layers are eroded away, the pressure on the underlying rock decreases. This reduction in pressure allows the rock to expand slightly, causing the outer layers to crack and peel away in sheets or slabs.

The process is often compared to peeling an onion, where each layer is removed to reveal a new surface. Chemical weathering also plays a role by weakening the bonds between mineral grains, making it easier for the rock layers to separate. This combination of pressure release and chemical weathering leads to the characteristic peeling effect observed in exfoliation.

## Related Terms

- **Weathering**: The breakdown of rocks and minerals at Earth's surface through physical, chemical, and biological processes.
- **Erosion**: The movement of rock particles by wind, water, ice, or gravity.
- **Chemical Weathering**: The process by which rocks are broken down by chemical reactions, often involving water and atmospheric gases.
- **Physical Weathering**: The mechanical breakdown of rocks into smaller fragments without changing their chemical composition.
- **Onion Skin Weathering**: Another term for exfoliation, emphasizing the peeling of rock layers similar to an onion's layers.
